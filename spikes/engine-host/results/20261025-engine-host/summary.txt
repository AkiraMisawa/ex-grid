== Reading ==

- The TypeScript engine against the C# engines, on these two scenarios. An edit that recalculates
  20,000 cells shows in 11 ms from the TypeScript Worker, against 556 ms from the C# Worker compiled
  ahead of time, 1,045 ms interpreted and 1,018 ms on published Blazor; an edit nothing reads, 5 ms
  against 11, 17 and 181. The pivot: a Change Batch of one trade 4.2 ms against 17, 37 and 24; the
  columns swapped 16 ms against 37, 120 and 140. Alone, the recalculation takes the TypeScript engine
  6 ms, and the C# engine 162 ms on the JIT, 525 AOT and 970 interpreted. The answers are the same,
  cell for cell (the check above).
- What that gap is, and is not. The TypeScript engine calculates Values and nothing else; the C# engine
  keeps, on every edit, what ExSheet needs beside them (the Step that undoes it, Spills, formats,
  volatile functions, the change named to the grid). So 6 ms is the floor of a TypeScript engine here,
  not what a port will take, and the 30 times between it and the C# JIT is not the language's: a C#
  engine written as the spike is was not measured. What the record does say is that the language and the
  runtime leave room: V8 runs the floor in 6 ms where .NET's WebAssembly runtimes take 0.5 to 1 s over
  the same cells. Exact decimal costs something even here: taking 20,000 P&Ls into base ten is 27 ms
  of the pivot's open, and a swap went from 10 to 13 ms when the sums became bigints.
- Does a TypeScript engine need a Worker? Not for these scenarios. On the page's own thread the edit of
  A1 blocks the page for 8 ms (worker column, page-ts) and shows in 15 ms; the swap blocks it for 14 ms;
  scrolling holds 17.9 ms frames (p95) against 17.3 in the Worker, and nothing recalculates while the
  driver scrolls. Each is within a frame. The open is 47 ms on the page. But this is the floor: a port
  that costs ten times the spike's recalculation (60 to 80 ms) drops three to five frames an edit on
  the page, and a Sheet fifty times this one costs fifty times more. So the evidence supports making
  the protocol the rule rather than the Worker: the engine reached only by asynchronous calls whose
  answers carry their version (spine rule 6), as here, where moving it between a Worker and the page
  changed no line of the pages; and a Worker where the real port measures an operation past a frame
  budget. ExPivot's redraws (under 14 ms on the page) need none at this size.
- Start. The TypeScript engine's page paints the Sheet in 0.17 to 0.19 s and the pivot in 0.19 to
  0.21 s, the whole download 58 KB (ExGrid's Script Core and the page about 52 KB, the engine about
  6 KB Brotli).
  The C# Worker downloads 2.74 MB interpreted and paints the Sheet in 5.0 s, 5.67 MB AOT and 2.0 s;
  published Blazor 4.01 MB and 5.0 s (the pivot 2.1 s). .NET starts in the Worker in 0.4 to 0.6 s;
  the rest is the first pass over the data: the Sheet's 20,000 Formulas take 4.5 s of engine time to
  open interpreted, 1.4 s AOT, 75 ms in TypeScript.
- Where Blazor WebAssembly's 3.3 s went. About three quarters of it was the baseline's configuration:
  Bench.Host under `dotnet run` serves the build output, whose .NET runtime is set up for debugging
  (debugLevel -1), and C# runs there two to five times slower. Published, the same page edits A1 in
  1,018 ms, not 4,069 ms (both in this container), and L1 in 181 ms, not 430. blazor-edit-trace.txt
  shows it step by step: Workbook.Do 3,507 ms in the build output and 784 ms published; ExGrid's
  PrepareRender 152 and 30; ToDocument 124 and 53.
- Of Blazor's published second, the engine is most (Workbook.Do 784 ms); ExSheet's Blazor side adds
  about 200 ms an edit whatever the edit reaches (SheetEdges.Update 26 ms over 20,000 Values, ExGrid's
  parameters and render about 100 ms, ToDocument 53 ms for the two-way binding, the page's render
  17 ms). That is why L1, which recalculates nothing, takes 181 ms on Blazor and 5 to 17 ms in any
  Worker arm.
- AOT against the interpreter, for the C# Worker: the recalculation about twice as fast (525 against
  970 ms), the pivot three to four times (a swap 26 against 94 ms), for twice the download (5.67
  against 2.74 MB, 4.5 MB of it dotnet.native.wasm). On the JIT the recalculation is 162 ms.

== What a full port would take ==

The TypeScript engine is 611 lines (ts-engine/sheet.ts and pivot.ts) for two scenarios. What it would
replace, in lines (not blank and not a comment in brackets):

  src/ExSheet.Engine      20,874 (13,303)   91 files, about 180 declared functions
  src/ExPivot.Engine      15,461 (10,872)
  src/ExGrid.Data          9,923  (7,195)   the Snapshot the pivot reads
  src/ExSheet.Xlsx         3,300  (2,298)
  src/ExGrid.Data.Xlsx     4,273  (3,245)
                          53,831 (36,913)   and src/ExChart.Engine, 5,888 (4,112), if it moves too

What proves a port answers as Excel does (ADR-0047, ADR-0060) does not need Excel run again: most of it
is data already, and the rest can be made data. In three parts:

  1. Run the Excel cases as they are. tests/ExSheet.Engine.Tests/ExcelCases: 3,365 cases in 163 area
     files and fixtures.json, 3,354 of them observed in Excel, their expectations mostly a Value
     (value2, 2,920) and otherwise the kind, the text shown, the Formula written back, the number
     format, digits, a refused entry, a width. ExcelOracle: 77 recorded runs of Excel, about 18,000 case
     results, under en-US, en-GB, de-DE and ja-JP. A TypeScript runner replaces ExcelCorpus.cs and
     ExcelCaseTests.cs (916 lines).
  2. Transcribe the remaining C# facts into shared cases (tests/cases/, layer 1's form under ADR-0350),
     so the C# engine passes them first and both engines run them while both exist:
       tests/ExSheet.Engine.Tests    18,390 lines, 1,125 Facts and Theories
       tests/ExPivot.Engine.Tests     9,024 lines,   351 (no Excel JSON, though ADR-0060's tables
                                                         were observed)
       tests/ExGrid.Data.Tests        5,670 lines,   273
       tests/ExSheet.Xlsx.Tests       3,537 lines,   174
       tests/ExGrid.Data.Xlsx.Tests   2,102 lines,    74
                                     38,723 lines, 1,997
  3. A differential fuzz against the C# engine where neither set reaches, above all:
     - decimal arithmetic: ExPivot sums a Decimal column exactly (decimal appears 93 times in
       ExPivot.Engine); JavaScript has no decimal, so the port chooses one (a library, or scaled bigint),
       which the Script Core API memo's number|bigint question touches. The spike holds 10^-10ths in a
       bigint, which covers its amounts and refuses finer ones; a port needs decimal's 28 digits and
       its scale (decimal keeps 1.0 apart from 1), which the spike does not;
     - Math functions near rounding boundaries (Formulas/CorrectlyRounded.cs,
       Functions.ObservedRounding.cs);
     - number to text: NumberText and NumberFormat answer as .NET's "G15" and .NET's culture data do;
       JavaScript's toPrecision and Intl (ICU) are not those. Two such facts surfaced while building
       the spike's few lines (the typed exponent, General's 1E-05).
     Beside them, what the runtime gave the C# engine for free and a port supplies: cultures (CultureInfo
     against Intl for en-GB, de-DE, ja-JP), dates (DateTime against Temporal), regular expressions
     (PortableRegex.cs exists because .NET's and JavaScript's differ), and the .xlsx readers' zip and XML.

For scale: ADR-0047's dated sections put the C# formula engine's building between 2026-09-27 and
2026-10-21, Excel runs included. A port writes the same behaviour again against cases that are ready,
without the Excel runs; part 2 is the larger task, since it turns 38,723 lines of C# tests into data.
