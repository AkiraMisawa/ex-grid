`MOD` gives Excel's digits where they were wrong: the remainder of Microsoft's `number - divisor*INT(number/divisor)` is now exact, as Excel's is, so `MOD(123456789012,0.7)` is 0.5000078322611261, where it gave 0.5000152587890625, `MOD(1,0.1)` and `MOD(5,0.1)` are 0.09999999999999995 and 0.09999999999999973, where they gave 0, `MOD(3.3,0.01)` is 0.009999999999999754, where it gave -4.440892098500626E-16, and `MOD(1.1,0.1)` is 2.7755575615628914E-17, where it gave 0. Where the divisor is added to a remainder of the other sign, the sum is held at 64 bits before it is stored as a double, which gives Excel's `MOD(-0.087626,6630)`, 6629.9123739999995, where it gave 6629.912374; and a sum stored as the divisor itself is 0, as Excel's `MOD(-1E-20,1)` is, where it gave 1. Where the evaluations at 64 bits that give every answer Excel was seen to give still part, `MOD` is `#VALUE!` until Excel is asked, where it gave a number: a sum that is the divisor as a double but not at 64 bits, as in `MOD(-1E-17,1)`, which gave 1, and a sum within a 64-bit unit of the midpoint between two doubles, as in `MOD(-0.084474,2270)`, which gave 2269.915526 — about one input of up to five digits in 150,000. A sum held at some other widths wider than a double's, which no hardware holds, would give every answer Excel was seen to give as well; where it would give another last digit, about 3 inputs of up to five digits in 100, as `MOD(-0.024901,2350)`, `MOD` answers as 64 bits do until Excel is asked.
